## 9. Optimization and Decision Support

### 9.1 From prediction to intervention

A forecast becomes useful only when it changes a decision. Decision support in a twin therefore wraps the predictive model in an optimisation or policy layer that searches the space of feasible interventions $\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}$ and evaluates their consequences through the model. The twin plays the role of a simulator inside the optimiser's loop, which is why the speed gains of Section 5.3 are critical. In receding-horizon form, the twin solves an optimisation over a horizon, applies the first action, observes the response and re-solves. This is model-predictive control with a continuously assimilated model.

### 9.2 Multi-objective optimization

Sustainability decisions trade objectives that cannot be reduced to a single currency without value judgements. The general problem is

$$
\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}} \; \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}) = \big[J_1(\mathbf{u}), J_2(\mathbf{u}), \ldots, J_M(\mathbf{u})\big]^{T} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g_q(\mathbf{u}) \le 0,\; q=1,\ldots,Q, \tag{16}
$$

where, for example, $J_1$ is operating cost, $J_2$ the carbon objective of Equation (15) and $J_3$ an unmet-service or discomfort index. A solution $\mathbf{u}^{a}$ *dominates* $\mathbf{u}^{b}$, written $\mathbf{u}^{a}\prec\mathbf{u}^{b}$, if $J_i(\mathbf{u}^{a}) \le J_i(\mathbf{u}^{b})$ for all $i$ and $J_j(\mathbf{u}^{a}) < J_j(\mathbf{u}^{b})$ for at least one $j$. The Pareto set is

$$
\mathcal{P}^{\ast} = \{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U} : \nexists\, \mathbf{u}'\in\mathcal{U} \text{ with } \mathbf{u}'\prec\mathbf{u}\}, \tag{17}
$$

and its image $\mathcal{F}^{\ast} = \{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}) : \mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}\}$ is the Pareto frontier. Evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II approximate the frontier with a population, using fast non-dominated sorting and a crowding-distance measure to maintain diversity [@deb]. Scalarisation methods offer an alternative: the weighted sum $\sum_i w_i J_i$ recovers only convex portions of the frontier, while the $\varepsilon$-constraint method, which minimises one objective subject to bounds on the others, recovers non-convex portions as well. Figure 3 illustrates a cost–emission frontier of the kind a twin presents to decision-makers.

![Figure 3. Conceptual cost–emission Pareto frontier produced by a digital twin (illustrative synthetic data). Filled markers denote non-dominated operating strategies; open markers denote dominated strategies; the knee point marks the region of greatest marginal trade-off.](figures/Figure3_Pareto_Frontier.png)

The frontier makes trade-offs explicit and leaves the choice of operating point to accountable decision-makers. Its *knee*, where small concessions in one objective buy large gains in another, is often a sensible default. Because the twin's objectives are uncertain, robust variants evaluate each candidate across the forecast ensemble and rank candidates by expected value, conditional value-at-risk or the probability of constraint violation.

### 9.3 Resource-efficiency optimization

Resource-efficiency objectives include water losses, energy per unit of service, such as kWh per cubic metre pumped or per square metre conditioned, and material throughput per unit of recovered value. Twins identify savings through operational adjustments such as pressure management, pump scheduling, set-point relaxation and route optimisation, which require no capital expenditure. They also identify savings through investment appraisal, in which the twin simulates the lifetime performance of candidate retrofits [@conejos; @crawley].

### 9.4 Emission and carbon optimization

Carbon objectives extend beyond operational emissions to embodied emissions of materials and equipment. A lifecycle-aware twin includes both, so that, for instance, the embodied carbon of additional battery capacity is weighed against the operational emissions it avoids. Time-varying grid intensity (Equation 15) makes temporal flexibility valuable, and twins quantify that value precisely.

### 9.5 Scenario analysis

Scenario analysis explores futures that cannot be assigned probabilities, such as climate pathways, policy regimes, technology costs and demand growth. The twin, initialised from its assimilated present state, is run under each scenario to test the robustness of candidate strategies. Strategies that perform acceptably across all scenarios are preferred to strategies that are optimal for one scenario and poor for others. This is the essence of decision-making under deep uncertainty.

### 9.6 Digital twins for policy and planning

At policy scale, twins become shared evidence platforms in which stakeholders can examine the consequences of proposals in a common, transparent model. Bauer et al. envisage Earth-scale twins as tools for exactly this purpose, supporting adaptation and mitigation decisions with physically consistent, high-resolution information [@bauer]. The governance requirements are substantial: model assumptions, data sources and uncertainties must be documented and open to challenge, and the twin must not be presented as more certain than it is.

## 10. Case Studies and Comparative Analysis

This section examines documented implementations across four domains and compares them using the DTCL taxonomy. The assessments of coupling level and maturity are the author's interpretation of the published descriptions, not claims made by the original authors.

### 10.1 Environmental monitoring

Earth-system twins exemplified by the Destination Earth initiative aim to merge kilometre-scale coupled models with continuous observation streams for weather extremes and climate adaptation [@bauer]. Their foundations, including global reanalysis such as ERA5 [@hersbach] and ensemble data assimilation [@evensen2009], are already operational. At this scale the twin's decision role is advisory; it informs policy and preparedness rather than actuating physical systems.

### 10.2 Urban sustainability

Urban twins range from three-dimensional city models enriched with sensor feeds to coupled simulations of energy, mobility and environment. Batty's critique remains pertinent: because urban dynamics are largely social, the twin's predictive authority is weaker than in engineered systems, and its most robust contributions are in physically governed sub-domains such as microclimate, flooding and energy [@batty]. Most urban twins currently sit at DTCL-2 to DTCL-3.

### 10.3 Energy systems

Building and district energy twins draw on mature simulation engines [@crawley] and on rich control-system data. They routinely reach DTCL-4, providing fault diagnosis and optimised schedules, and in microgrids with automated dispatch they reach DTCL-5 (Figure 2). Their principal challenges are model calibration under occupant variability and interoperability across building-management vendors.

### 10.4 Water systems

The Valencia distribution-network twin is a well-documented example of a utility-scale twin used for planning, operation and maintenance [@conejos]. Water systems benefit from long traditions of hydraulic modelling and SCADA, which ease the transition from shadow to twin. Agricultural water twins form a parallel strand, integrating soil and crop sensing with irrigation control [@verdouw].

### 10.5 Comparison of implementation strategies

Table 2 synthesises the cases.

**Table 2. Evaluation matrix of documented digital-twin implementations for sustainable systems.**

| Domain / case | Physical scope | Principal data streams | Modelling approach | Decision output | Assessed DTCL | Key limitation | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earth-system twin (Destination Earth vision) | Global coupled atmosphere–ocean–land | Satellite, in-situ, reanalysis | Km-scale physics models with data assimilation | Climate-impact and extreme-event information for policy | DTCL-3 to DTCL-4 (advisory) | Extreme computational cost | [@bauer; @hersbach] |

Three patterns emerge from Table 2. First, coupling level tracks the maturity of the underlying physics and the controllability of the system: engineered systems with good models and actuators reach DTCL-4 or DTCL-5, while natural and social systems rarely pass DTCL-4. Second, every case at DTCL-3 or above depends on a mechanistic model, often accelerated or corrected by learning; purely data-driven twins rarely appear in documented operational deployments. Third, the limiting factor shifts with scale, from calibration and data heterogeneity at asset scale to computational cost at planetary scale.

## 11. Challenges and Research Gaps

The obstacles to trustworthy, sustainable twins are summarised in Table 3 and discussed below.

### 11.1 Interoperability

Twins integrate equipment, software and data from many vendors and organisations, and the lack of standardisation is consistently reported as a barrier to their adoption [@fuller; @jones]. Without shared information models every integration is bespoke. OPC UA provides a platform-independent, service-oriented architecture with typed information models, and the Asset Administration Shell, developed within the German Industrie 4.0 programme, offers a standardised digital representation of assets with submodels for properties and capabilities. Extending such standards to environmental and urban domains, where assets include rivers and neighbourhoods rather than machines, remains an open task.

### 11.2 Data quality and availability

Observability gaps, sensor drift and inconsistent metadata limit what any twin can know. Research is needed on sensor-network design that targets posterior uncertainty in decision-relevant quantities, and on error models for low-cost sensors that can be estimated online.

### 11.3 Computational requirements

Ensemble assimilation and robust optimisation multiply model cost by ensemble size and by the number of candidate decisions. Reduced-order and operator-learning surrogates relieve this burden [@berkooz; @lu], but their errors must be tracked and propagated, not ignored.

### 11.4 Cybersecurity and privacy

Bidirectional coupling exposes the physical system to attacks through the virtual one. Alcaraz and Lopez classify threats to twins by functional layer and highlight risks including data manipulation, model poisoning and compromise of the control channel [@alcaraz]. At DTCL-5 a falsified state estimate becomes a falsified actuation. Privacy is a parallel concern wherever twins use smart-meter, mobility or occupancy data that reveal personal behaviour.

### 11.5 Validation and verification

Verification confirms that software solves the intended equations; validation confirms that the model represents reality adequately for its purpose. For adaptive twins, validation cannot be a one-off certification, because the model changes continuously. Continuous validation through held-out observations, innovation statistics (Section 6.5) and post-decision outcome analysis is needed, together with accepted criteria for adequacy.

### 11.6 Explainability and trust

Operators must understand why a twin recommends an action before they will act on it. Post-hoc attribution methods such as SHAP, which assigns each input a contribution derived from Shapley values with consistency guarantees [@lundberg], can explain learned components, while physics-based structure provides intrinsic interpretability. Uncertainty displays help calibrate trust, guarding against both rejection and over-reliance.

### 11.7 Long-term sustainability of digital infrastructure

Sensors, networks, servers and model training consume energy and materials. A twin whose computing footprint exceeds the savings it delivers is not sustainable. Lifecycle accounting of the twin itself, and right-sizing of model fidelity and update frequency to decision needs, should be standard practice.

### 11.8 Human-in-the-loop decision-making

Autonomy should be granted incrementally and conditionally. Governed autonomy, in which the twin acts automatically within approved envelopes and escalates beyond them, preserves human accountability while realising the speed benefits of automation. Decision logs that record the state estimate, forecast, alternatives and chosen action support both learning and accountability.

## 12. Future Directions

### 12.1 Autonomous and adaptive digital twins

The trajectory of the field is up the DTCL ladder, as Figure 4 shows. Progress from DTCL-3 to DTCL-5 will depend on adaptive learning that detects and accommodates drift without human intervention [@gama], combined with formal guarantees on safe behaviour during adaptation.

![Figure 4. Maturity pathway of the Digital Twin Coupling Level (DTCL) taxonomy proposed in this chapter. Each level is cumulative; the enabling capability required to advance is shown beneath each step.](figures/Figure4_DTCL_Maturity_Pathway.png)

### 12.2 Edge–cloud digital-twin architectures

Partitioning computation between edge devices, which are close to sensors and actuators and offer low latency, and cloud resources, which are elastic and suit heavy simulation, allows twins to meet both real-time and high-fidelity requirements [@fuller]. Open questions include adaptive placement of model components and consistency management between edge and cloud states.

### 12.3 Physics-informed digital twins

Physics-informed learning [@raissi; @karniadakis] and neural operators [@lu] promise twins that are both fast and physically consistent. Key research needs include robust training for stiff and multiscale problems, uncertainty quantification for physics-informed models, and certified error bounds.

### 12.4 Foundation models and emerging AI methods

Large pre-trained models of Earth-system and engineering data could provide transferable representations that reduce the data needed to build new twins [@reichstein]. Their use in twins will require careful treatment of physical consistency, uncertainty and domain shift.

### 12.5 Multi-system and city-scale twins

Federated twins, in which independently owned twins of water, energy, transport and buildings exchange information through standard interfaces, can capture cross-sector interactions without centralising data or control [@batty; @fuller].

### 12.6 Digital twins for climate resilience

Twins that combine climate projections with infrastructure models can stress-test systems against future extremes, prioritise adaptation investments and coordinate emergency response [@bauer]. They extend the twin's horizon from operations to long-term planning.

## 13. Conclusions

This chapter has argued that a digital twin for sustainable systems is defined by the closure of a statistically rigorous information loop between a specific physical system and its virtual representation, not by visual fidelity or connectivity alone. Building on established classifications of models, shadows and twins [@kritzinger], it introduced the six-level Digital Twin Coupling Level taxonomy. The taxonomy separates streaming shadows from assimilating twins, and advisory twins from autonomous ones, by the direction, latency and statistical treatment of information flow.

A six-tier reference architecture (Figure 1) organises the technologies involved and frames the twin as a feedback system subject to the familiar demands of stability, robustness and delay. Data assimilation, and the ensemble Kalman filter in particular, was presented as the mechanism that converts a shadow into a twin. Attention was given to matrix dimensions, ensemble covariance propagation and the rank deficiency that localisation and inflation must address [@evensen1994; @evensen2009]. The comparison of physics-based, data-driven and hybrid strategies (Table 1) showed that hybrid and physics-informed approaches, supported by reduced-order models and neural operators, offer the most credible route to fast, physically consistent and ultimately autonomous twins [@raissi; @berkooz; @lu].

Uncertainty runs through every function: calibration with explicit model discrepancy [@kennedy], Monte Carlo and Bayesian propagation, and drift detection through innovation statistics [@gama]. Applications in water, air, circular resources, ecosystems, agriculture, buildings, renewables and grids demonstrate breadth. The comparative analysis (Table 2) shows that coupling level tracks the maturity of the physics and the controllability of the system. Multi-objective optimisation (Figure 3) turns predictions into transparent trade-offs between cost, carbon and service.

The challenges summarised in Table 3, namely interoperability, data quality, computation, cybersecurity [@alcaraz], continuous validation, explainability [@lundberg], the footprint of the digital infrastructure itself and human oversight, are substantial but tractable. Progress up the coupling ladder (Figure 4) should be governed by evidence that each new level of autonomy is warranted. Twins built on these principles can become durable instruments for operating and planning the resource, energy and environmental systems on which a sustainable future depends.
