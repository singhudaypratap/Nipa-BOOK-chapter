## 5. Physics-Based, Data-Driven and Hybrid Digital Twins

### 5.1 Physics-based modelling

Physics-based twins encode conservation laws and constitutive relations. For flow-dominated environmental systems such as urban airflow, pollutant dispersion, rivers and coastal waters, the governing equations are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations together with an advection–diffusion–reaction equation for a transported scalar $c$:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c = \nabla\cdot(D\nabla c) + R(c) + S, \tag{7}
$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is velocity, $p$ pressure, $\rho$ density, $\nu$ kinematic viscosity, $\mathbf{f}$ body forces, $D$ diffusivity, $R$ a reaction term and $S$ a source. Analogous balance laws govern heat transfer in buildings, charge and mass transport in batteries, and water and solute movement in soils. The strengths of mechanistic models are extrapolative credibility, since conservation holds outside the range of past data, and interpretability, since every parameter has physical meaning. Their weaknesses are computational cost, sensitivity to poorly known parameters and boundary conditions, and structural error where processes are parameterised rather than resolved [@rasheed].

### 5.2 Statistical and machine-learning models

Data-driven twins learn input–output relationships directly from observations. Approaches range from autoregressive and Gaussian-process models to gradient-boosted trees and deep neural networks capable of representing complex spatiotemporal dependence [@reichstein]. They are fast at inference, need no explicit process description and can exploit high-dimensional covariates. Their limitations are equally clear: they need large, representative training sets, they offer no guarantee of physical consistency, and their behaviour outside the training distribution is unreliable. Reichstein et al. argue that in Earth-system applications these limitations are fundamental rather than incidental, and that process understanding must be combined with learning [@reichstein].

### 5.3 Reduced-order and surrogate models

Operational twins frequently need many model evaluations, for ensemble assimilation, uncertainty propagation or optimisation, within tight time budgets. Reduced-order models (ROMs) supply this speed. In proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), $N_s$ snapshots of a high-fidelity solution are assembled into a matrix $\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{x}(t_1),\ldots,\mathbf{x}(t_{N_s})] \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times N_s}$, whose thin singular-value decomposition is

$$
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}. \tag{8}
$$

The leading $r \ll n$ left singular vectors $\boldsymbol{\Phi}_r = [\boldsymbol{\phi}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{\phi}_r]$ form an orthonormal basis that is optimal in the least-squares sense, capturing a fraction $\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sigma_j^2 / \sum_{j}\sigma_j^2$ of snapshot energy [@berkooz]. Approximating $\mathbf{x}(t) \approx \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \boldsymbol{\Phi}_r\mathbf{a}(t)$ and Galerkin-projecting the full model $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})$ onto the basis yields an $r$-dimensional system

$$
\dot{\mathbf{a}} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_r^{T}\,\mathbf{F}\!\left(\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \boldsymbol{\Phi}_r\mathbf{a}\right), \tag{9}
$$

which can be integrated orders of magnitude faster when the nonlinear term is evaluated efficiently. Non-intrusive surrogates avoid projection altogether by learning the map from inputs to outputs, or to POD coefficients, with regression or neural networks. Neural operators go further and learn mappings between function spaces. DeepONet, for instance, approximates a nonlinear operator with a branch network that encodes the input function and a trunk network that encodes the output location [@lu]. Surrogates must be validated over the region of input space where the twin will be operated, and their approximation error should be fed into the twin's uncertainty budget.

### 5.4 Hybrid physics–data-driven modelling

Hybrid models place learned components where physics is weak and keep mechanistic structure where it is strong [@rasheed; @reichstein]. Common patterns include: (i) *residual learning*, in which a network predicts the discrepancy between a mechanistic model and observations; (ii) *learned parameterisation*, in which a sub-grid closure or empirical coefficient is replaced by a network embedded in the solver; (iii) *serial coupling*, in which a physical model generates features that a statistical model maps to the quantity of interest; and (iv) *learned dynamics with physical priors*, in which a network's architecture or loss enforces known invariants. Hybrids generally extrapolate better than pure learners and fit better than pure mechanistic models, but they introduce new failure modes, such as unstable feedback between learned and physical components during long integrations.

### 5.5 Physics-informed machine learning

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) embed governing equations in the training objective [@raissi]. Let $u_\theta(\mathbf{x},t)$ be a network with parameters $\theta$ approximating the solution of a partial differential equation written in residual form

$$
\mathcal{N}[u](\mathbf{x},t) \equiv \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}[u] - s(\mathbf{x},t) = 0, \qquad (\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T], \tag{10}
$$

where $\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}$ is a possibly nonlinear spatial differential operator with physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$; for Equation (7), $\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}[c] = \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c - \nabla\cdot(D\nabla c) - R(c)$. The PINN is trained by minimising

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\theta) = w_d\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + w_p\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} + w_b\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}}, \tag{11}
$$

with

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} = \frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\left|u_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i,t_i) - z_i\right|^2,\quad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{j=1}^{N_r}\left|\mathcal{N}[u_\theta](\mathbf{x}_j,t_j)\right|^2,\quad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}} = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{l=1}^{N_b}\left|\mathcal{B}[u_\theta](\mathbf{x}_l,t_l) - g_l\right|^2. \tag{12}
$$

Here $z_i$ are observations, $(\mathbf{x}_j,t_j)$ are collocation points at which the residual is evaluated by automatic differentiation, and $\mathcal{B}$ encodes boundary and initial conditions with prescribed values $g_l$. Because $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ can be included among the trainable variables, the same formulation solves inverse problems, for example inferring a spatially varying diffusivity from sparse concentration measurements [@raissi; @karniadakis]. The weights $w_d$, $w_p$ and $w_b$ balance terms whose magnitudes and gradient scales may differ by orders of magnitude; poor balancing is a known cause of training failure, and adaptive weighting schemes have been proposed to address it [@karniadakis]. For digital twins, the attraction of PINNs is that they assimilate sparse, noisy data while respecting conservation, and that a trained network evaluates rapidly at arbitrary points.

### 5.6 Choosing the appropriate modelling strategy

No single paradigm dominates. Table 1 compares the three families along criteria relevant to sustainable twins.

**Table 1. Comparative matrix of physics-based, data-driven and hybrid digital twins.**

|---|---|---|---|
| Core representation | Conservation laws, constitutive relations (Eqs. 6–7) | Statistical or neural mappings learned from data | Mechanistic core with learned closures, residuals or physics-penalised losses (Eqs. 11–12) |

| Typical DTCL reached | DTCL-3 to DTCL-4 with ROMs | DTCL-2 to DTCL-4 | DTCL-3 to DTCL-5 |

Three observations follow from Table 1. First, the choice is driven by the availability of reliable physics relative to the availability of representative data: well-understood hydraulics favour mechanistic models, while poorly characterised processes such as occupant behaviour favour learning. Second, inference speed matters only relative to the decision time scale; a CFD model that runs in an hour is adequate for daily planning but useless for real-time control unless it is reduced. Third, the hybrid column is not a compromise but frequently the only route to DTCL-5 autonomy, because autonomous control demands both speed and trustworthy behaviour under novel conditions.

## 6. Prediction, Uncertainty and Model Updating

### 6.1 State prediction

State prediction, or *nowcasting* in the twin's own terms, is the estimation of the full current state from partial observations. It is the output of the analysis step in Section 4.6 and is the foundation for every downstream function. Its quality is measured not only by error against held-out sensors but by the reliability of its uncertainty: the spread of the analysis ensemble should match the actual error statistics.

### 6.2 Forecasting future system behaviour

Forecasting propagates the analysed state forward under assumed or forecast drivers. For an ensemble twin this means integrating every member to the forecast horizon, so that forecast uncertainty grows naturally through nonlinear dynamics. Forecast skill degrades with lead time at rates set by the system's predictability: grid load is predictable over hours to days, river discharge over days to weeks depending on catchment memory, and groundwater over months. Twins that serve multiple decision horizons often combine a fast data-driven short-range forecaster with a mechanistic medium-range model.

### 6.3 Model calibration and parameter estimation

Calibration adjusts model parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ so that outputs match observations. In the Bayesian framework of Kennedy and O'Hagan, observations are modelled as

$$
z(\mathbf{x}) = \eta(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}) + \delta(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon, \tag{13}
$$

where $\eta$ is the simulator at the true but unknown parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}$, $\delta$ is a model-discrepancy function, usually given a Gaussian-process prior, and $\epsilon$ is observation noise [@kennedy]. The explicit discrepancy term is important: without it, calibration forces parameters to compensate for structural error, producing values that fit the calibration period but generalise poorly. In operational twins, calibration is performed offline for slowly varying parameters and online, through state augmentation in the EnKF, for parameters expected to drift.

### 6.4 Uncertainty propagation

Uncertainty arises from inputs, parameters, model structure, initial conditions and observations. Monte Carlo propagation samples these sources and runs the model for each sample; the empirical distribution of outputs approximates the predictive distribution, with sampling error decreasing as $N^{-1/2}$ independently of dimension. Its cost motivates surrogates (Section 5.3) and variance-reduction techniques such as Latin hypercube sampling. Bayesian methods instead seek the posterior $p(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) \propto p(\mathbf{z}\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{x})\,p(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{x})$, by Markov chain Monte Carlo when the model is cheap or emulated, or by ensemble approximations in sequential settings [@kennedy; @evensen2009]. Whatever the method, the twin should report predictive intervals, not point values, to the decision layer, and decisions should be evaluated against the full distribution of outcomes.

### 6.5 Model drift and changing environmental conditions

Drift has two sources. *Physical drift* occurs when the system itself changes: pipes corrode, heat exchangers foul, catchment land cover changes, climate shifts. *Statistical drift*, or concept drift, occurs when the joint distribution of inputs and outputs that a learned component was trained on no longer holds [@gama]. Gama et al. distinguish sudden, gradual, incremental and recurring drift and survey detectors based on monitoring error rates or distributional statistics [@gama]. For twins, a practical indicator is the sequence of normalised innovations, $\mathbf{d}_k = \mathbf{S}_k^{-1/2}(\mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}_k\mathbf{x}_k^f)$ with $\mathbf{S}_k = \mathbf{H}_k\mathbf{P}_k^f\mathbf{H}_k^T + \mathbf{R}_k$. Under a correct model these are approximately standard normal and serially uncorrelated; persistent bias or inflated variance signals drift.

### 6.6 Continuous model updating

Continuous updating closes the adaptive loop of Section 2.5. It operates at three rates: state updates at every assimilation cycle; parameter updates over days to months via augmented-state filtering or rolling recalibration; and structural updates, such as retraining a learned component or adding a process, triggered by drift detection. Each update should be versioned, with the data, code and configuration that produced it, so that any past recommendation can be reproduced. Without such provenance, an adaptive twin cannot be audited and therefore cannot be trusted with consequential decisions.

## 7. Digital Twins for Environmental and Resource Systems

### 7.1 Water-resource systems

Water distribution and catchment management are among the most mature twin domains, because hydraulic and hydrological models are well established and utilities already operate extensive telemetry. Conejos Fuertes et al. describe the construction and exploitation of a digital twin of the drinking-water distribution network of Valencia, Spain, built on a detailed EPANET hydraulic model kept up to date with operational data and used for planning, operation and maintenance tasks [@conejos]. Typical functions include leak localisation from pressure and flow anomalies, pump scheduling to reduce energy use, and water-age and chlorine-decay estimation for quality management. At catchment scale, twins combine rainfall–runoff models, reservoir operations and assimilated discharge and soil-moisture observations to support flood control and allocation decisions.

### 7.2 Air-quality monitoring

Urban air quality is governed by emissions, meteorology and chemistry interacting at street to regional scales. An air-quality twin couples a dispersion model, from Gaussian plume to CFD depending on scale (Equations 6–7), with regulatory monitors and dense low-cost sensor networks. Assimilation corrects emission inventories and boundary conditions, while the differing error characteristics of reference and low-cost instruments are encoded in $\mathbf{R}_k$. The twin can then evaluate interventions such as traffic restrictions or low-emission zones before they are imposed.

### 7.3 Waste and circular-resource systems

In waste and circular-resource systems, the state of interest is a set of material stocks and flows rather than a physical field. Twins of collection networks use fill-level sensors and vehicle telemetry to optimise routing, while twins of material-recovery facilities track composition and throughput to maintain sorting quality. At broader scale, a material-flow twin links product, component and material inventories with lifecycle data, so that end-of-life recovery can be planned. Mass balance is the governing constraint, and it offers a natural physics term for hybrid models.

### 7.4 Ecosystem and environmental monitoring

Ecosystem twins combine process models of vegetation, carbon, water and nutrients with remote-sensing and in-situ observations. The Destination Earth vision of a high-resolution Earth twin illustrates the ambition: kilometre-scale simulation of the coupled Earth system, continuously informed by observations and accessible for impact and adaptation studies [@bauer]. At smaller scales, twins of wetlands, forests or coastal zones support habitat management, carbon accounting and early warning of degradation.

### 7.5 Agricultural systems

Verdouw et al. present a conceptual framework and architecture for digital twins in smart farming, covering plants, animals, fields and farm operations, and they classify twin types including monitoring, predictive, prescriptive and autonomous twins [@verdouw]. Agricultural twins integrate soil-moisture probes, weather stations, satellite vegetation indices and crop models to schedule irrigation, fertilisation and harvest. Their sustainability benefits come from reduced water, energy and agrochemical inputs per unit of yield.

### 7.6 Urban environmental systems

Cities are systems of systems, and city-scale twins must integrate buildings, transport, utilities, open space and people. Batty observes that the urban twin raises distinctive questions because cities are not machines with fixed specifications: their behaviour emerges from human decisions, and a twin can only partially mirror them [@batty]. Urban environmental twins nevertheless deliver value in heat-island analysis, stormwater management, noise and air-quality planning, and coordinated infrastructure investment, provided their limits are made explicit.

## 8. Digital Twins for Energy and Built Environments

### 8.1 Buildings and energy consumption

Buildings are well suited to twinning because their physics is mature and their control systems already expose data. Whole-building simulation engines such as EnergyPlus integrate heat-balance, airflow, plant and system models to compute loads and energy use [@crawley]. A building twin calibrates such a model, or a reduced resistance–capacitance surrogate, to metered data and indoor-climate sensors, then uses it for fault detection, ventilation and set-point optimisation, and retrofit appraisal. The lumped thermal balance for a zone of capacitance $C$,

$$
C\frac{dT_{\text{in}}}{dt} = \frac{T_{\text{out}} - T_{\text{in}}}{R} + \dot{Q}_{\text{HVAC}} + \dot{Q}_{\text{solar}} + \dot{Q}_{\text{int}}, \tag{14}
$$

is a typical physics term for hybrid twins, in which $R$ and $C$ are estimated online and learned components capture occupancy-driven gains $\dot{Q}_{\text{int}}$.

### 8.2 Renewable-energy systems

Wind and solar plants benefit from twins that combine physical performance models with assimilation of measured output. For wind turbines, twins track component fatigue and wake interactions; for photovoltaic arrays, they separate soiling, degradation and shading losses. Forecasting of resource availability, coupled to the asset model, supports market bidding and maintenance planning.

### 8.3 Energy-grid integration

As variable renewables displace synchronous generation, grid operators face reduced inertia, bidirectional flows in distribution networks and rapid ramps. Grid twins solve power-flow and dynamic-stability models on state estimates derived from phasor and smart-meter measurements, and evaluate contingencies faster than real time. At distribution level, they coordinate distributed energy resources, electric-vehicle charging and storage within voltage and thermal limits.

### 8.4 District and urban energy systems

District heating and cooling networks, multi-energy hubs and urban microgrids couple thermal, electrical and sometimes gas carriers. Their twins must represent conversion technologies such as heat pumps, combined heat and power units and storage, together with network transport delays. Figure 2 shows the feedback loop of a microgrid twin, which is representative of this class.

![Figure 2. Feedback loop of a microgrid energy digital twin. Measurements from generation, storage and loads are assimilated into the virtual microgrid; forecasts feed a carbon-aware optimiser whose dispatch set-points are applied to the physical assets and observed again.](figures/Figure2_Microgrid_Feedback_Loop.png)

In this loop, the physical microgrid, comprising photovoltaic generation, battery storage, controllable loads and the grid connection, is observed by meters and inverters. The observations update the virtual microgrid state, including battery state of charge and load estimates, through assimilation. The updated state initialises forecasts of generation and demand, which feed an optimiser that also receives grid carbon-intensity and price signals. The resulting dispatch set-points are applied to inverters and controllable loads, and their effects are measured at the next cycle. The loop is an instance of DTCL-5 operation when set-points are applied automatically within approved bounds.

### 8.5 Carbon-aware operation and optimization

Carbon-aware operation shifts flexible demand and storage toward periods of low marginal emission intensity. Formally, for dispatch decisions $\mathbf{p}_t$ over horizon $T$, the operational carbon objective is

$$
J_{\text{CO}_2} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \gamma_t\, P^{\text{grid}}_t(\mathbf{p}_t)\,\Delta t, \tag{15}
$$

where $\gamma_t$ is the forecast marginal carbon intensity of imported electricity (kg CO$_2$ kWh$^{-1}$) and $P^{\text{grid}}_t$ is net import. Because $\gamma_t$ and the renewable output are forecasts, the twin's uncertainty estimates enter the optimisation directly, either as scenarios or as chance constraints. This objective is generally in tension with cost and comfort, which leads naturally to the multi-objective formulation of Section 9.
