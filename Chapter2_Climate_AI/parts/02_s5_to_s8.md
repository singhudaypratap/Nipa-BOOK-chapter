## 5. AI Applications in Climate Prediction

### 5.1 Temperature prediction

Near-surface and upper-air temperature are the most predictable variables because they are smooth in space and strongly constrained by large-scale dynamics. Global data-driven models trained on ERA5 now produce multi-day temperature forecasts that compete with operational numerical systems [@pathak; @lam]. WeatherBench defines the standard evaluation, in which 500 hPa geopotential height and 850 hPa temperature are forecast at lead times of several days and scored by latitude-weighted root-mean-square error [@rasp2020]. At seasonal to decadal scales, skill derives from slowly varying boundary conditions, chiefly ocean temperature, and recurrent or convolutional networks are used to exploit that memory. Whatever the horizon, a forecast must be compared with the right baseline: persistence and climatology at short leads, and damped persistence or linear inverse models at seasonal leads.

### 5.2 Precipitation and rainfall forecasting

Precipitation is intermittent, skewed and spatially discontinuous, so squared-error losses applied to it produce blurred forecasts that underestimate extremes. ConvLSTM was developed for radar-based nowcasting over a few hours and demonstrated gains over optical-flow extrapolation on that task [@shi]. At longer leads, global models learn precipitation less reliably than state variables because precipitation is a diagnosed, parameterised quantity in the reanalysis they imitate [@hersbach; @rasp2020]. Remedies include zero-inflated or gamma likelihoods, quantile and distributional losses, and adversarial training, all aimed at preserving the sharpness and tail of the distribution.

### 5.3 Drought prediction

Drought is a slow, cumulative anomaly of the water balance, and is quantified through indices. The Standardised Precipitation Index (SPI) fits a probability distribution, typically a gamma, to accumulated precipitation over a chosen window and transforms the cumulative probability to a standard normal variate. The Standardised Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) applies the same procedure to the climatic water balance $D=P-\mathrm{PET}$, which includes the temperature-driven evaporative demand and therefore responds to warming [@vicente]. Because indices are autocorrelated and driven by accumulated anomalies, tree ensembles and LSTMs given antecedent index values, soil-moisture and teleconnection predictors perform well at one-to-few-month leads. Skill beyond that depends largely on the predictability of the driving ocean state.

### 5.4 Flood and hydrological extremes

Flood prediction links meteorological forcing to catchment response. Recurrent networks trained on rainfall–runoff data have been shown to perform strongly in streamflow prediction, in part because the LSTM cell state acts as a learnable store of snow and soil-water memory [@hochreiter]. The difficulty is extremes: floods are rare, so training sets contain few examples, and the largest events lie outside the observed range. Hybrid approaches embed a conceptual water balance inside the network, or use the network to predict parameters of a hydrological model, trading some flexibility for plausible behaviour at high flows.

### 5.5 Heatwave prediction

Heatwaves are defined by thresholds on temperature persistence, for instance exceedance of a local percentile for several consecutive days. They are therefore a classification or event-probability problem built on a continuous variable. Predictability comes from persistent anticyclonic blocking, soil-moisture deficits that amplify surface heating, and sea-surface temperature patterns. Models that take soil moisture and 500 hPa geopotential anomalies as inputs and predict exceedance probability can provide advance warning at lead times of days to weeks, and the main evaluation concern is calibration of those probabilities and the balance between missed events and false alarms.

### 5.6 Cyclones and severe weather

Tropical cyclone track forecasting has benefited from learned global models. GraphCast, evaluated on tropical cyclone tracks, reports lower track error than a leading operational model across lead times [@lam]. Intensity is harder, since it depends on inner-core processes that 0.25° grids do not resolve and on rapid intensification, a notoriously sensitive phenomenon. Learned detectors of cyclones and atmospheric rivers in reanalysis and climate-model output provide census statistics of how events change under warming, though detection thresholds must be consistent between the datasets compared.

### 5.7 Greenhouse-gas and carbon-emission prediction

Predicting concentrations and emissions combines physical and socio-economic data. Atmospheric concentration depends on emissions, land and ocean sinks and transport, while emissions depend on energy demand, fuel mix and policy. Machine-learning models are used to nowcast sectoral emissions from activity data, to estimate fluxes from satellite column retrievals, and to forecast electricity demand and carbon intensity for grid operators [@rolnick]. Long-horizon emissions projections are better treated as scenario analysis, using the SSP framework [@eyring], than as statistical extrapolation, because policy and technology shifts break the historical relationships a model has learned.

First, the metric must match the decision: RMSE rewards smooth forecasts that miss extremes, whereas threat scores and the continuous ranked probability score reward discrimination and calibration. Second, difficulty follows predictability, not model sophistication. Where the physical signal is weak or the target is rare, a more elaborate network does not create information that is not in the data.

## 6. Climate Downscaling and Regional Prediction

### 6.1 Global versus regional climate information

Adaptation decisions are local, but global models resolve scales of tens to hundreds of kilometres. Mountains, coastlines, lakes and cities modulate temperature and precipitation at scales below the grid, and extreme events are controlled by processes that coarse models parameterise. Downscaling bridges the gap, either dynamically, by running a regional model nested in the global one, or statistically, by learning a mapping from coarse predictors to fine-scale predictands.

### 6.2 Statistical downscaling

Classical statistical downscaling assumes that local weather is conditioned by the large-scale state. It takes three forms: regression relating predictors to local variables, weather-typing approaches that resample local weather conditional on circulation regime, and stochastic weather generators. All rest on the stationarity assumption that the relationship estimated from historical data remains valid under future forcing. This assumption cannot be verified with observations alone and is the main conceptual risk of the approach.

### 6.3 Machine-learning-based downscaling

Machine learning replaces linear transfer functions with flexible nonlinear ones. A downscaling network maps a coarse field $\mathbf{X}_c$ and static high-resolution covariates $\mathbf{S}$ (orography, land-sea mask, land cover) to a fine field $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_f$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_f = f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_c,\mathbf{S}), \qquad \theta^{\ast}=\arg\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}\big[\ell(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_f,\mathbf{Y}_f)\big]. \tag{4}
$$

The choice of loss determines the character of the output. A mean-squared-error loss yields the conditional mean, which is smooth, and underestimates variance and extremes. Likelihood-based, adversarial or diffusion-style losses sample from the conditional distribution and restore realistic variability, at the price of outputs that are not unique for a given input.

### 6.4 Spatial super-resolution approaches

Super-resolution treats a coarse field as a low-resolution image to be upsampled. DeepSD applied stacked super-resolution CNNs to downscale precipitation over the United States, learning successive resolution increases with topography as auxiliary input and comparing favourably with several statistical downscaling benchmarks [@vandal]. Generative adversarial networks add a discriminator that penalises unrealistic texture and so produce sharper fields. ### 6.5 Bias correction

Climate-model output systematically departs from observations, and downstream impact models are sensitive to such departures. Quantile mapping corrects the distribution by transforming model values through the mapping

$$
x_{\text{corr}} = F_{o}^{-1}\big(F_{m}(x_{m})\big), \tag{5}
$$

where $F_m$ is the cumulative distribution function of the model in the reference period and $F_o^{-1}$ the quantile function of observations. Cannon et al. showed that simple quantile mapping can distort the climate-change signal in quantiles and extremes, since it forces the corrected historical distribution to match observations whatever the projected change, and proposed trend-preserving variants [@cannon]. Bias correction should therefore be applied with awareness that it modifies not only the mean state but the projected change itself.

### 6.6 Regional transferability

A model trained on one region is often applied to another with sparser data. Transferability depends on whether the physical regime is shared: a network trained over temperate mid-latitudes may fail in the tropics, where convective rather than frontal precipitation dominates. Common practice is to fine-tune a pre-trained model on a small regional dataset, to include static covariates that let the network condition on local geography, or to train on pooled multi-region data. Transfer should be tested by withholding entire regions, not random grid cells (Section 10.2).

### 6.7 Limitations of AI-generated high-resolution climate information

A downscaled field has more pixels but not necessarily more information. The network can only add fine-scale structure that is predictable from the coarse input and static covariates; beyond that it adds plausible texture. Users must distinguish *deterministic* detail, such as orographic enhancement of rainfall, from *stochastic* detail, which is statistically realistic but not tied to any particular event. Further limits are stationarity, since the learned relationship may change under warming, and physical consistency, since independently downscaled variables need not satisfy the relations that bind them. High resolution should never be mistaken for high confidence.

## 7. Hybrid and Physics-Informed Climate AI

### 7.1 Why purely data-driven prediction can fail

Free-running neural emulators are unconstrained by physics. Small violations of mass or energy conservation can accumulate over thousands of autoregressive steps and produce drift, spurious trends or unphysical states such as negative humidity. Models trained for short-lead accuracy may produce physically implausible fields on long rollouts even when each step looks accurate. The governing primitive equations make the constraints explicit. For an atmosphere in hydrostatic balance on a sphere, mass continuity and momentum conservation read

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,\qquad
\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p-2\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{F}, \tag{6}
$$

together with the thermodynamic energy equation $c_p\,DT/Dt-\alpha\,Dp/Dt=Q$ and the equation of state, where $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is Earth's rotation vector, $\mathbf{g}$ gravity, $\mathbf{F}$ friction and $Q$ diabatic heating. A network that ignores these relations has no reason to satisfy them.

### 7.2 Incorporating physical constraints

Constraints may be *soft* or *hard*. A soft constraint adds a penalty to the loss, for example the squared violation of column-integrated moisture or energy balance; it is easy to implement but the constraint is satisfied only approximately and only on the training distribution. A hard constraint builds satisfaction into the architecture, for instance by predicting a stream function so that the resulting velocity is divergence-free by construction, or by adding a final layer that rescales outputs to enforce a conserved budget exactly. Hard constraints guarantee the property but may restrict expressiveness, and designing them demands knowledge of which invariants matter [@kashinath; @reichstein].

### 7.3 Hybrid numerical–AI models

Hybrid models keep the dynamical core of a numerical model and replace uncertain components with learned ones. The most developed example is the sub-grid parameterisation. Rasp et al. trained a deep neural network on high-resolution simulations with explicitly resolved convection to predict sub-grid heating and moistening tendencies from the coarse-grained state, and showed that the network, embedded in a coarse model, reproduced the mean climate and aspects of variability at a fraction of the cost [@rasp2018]. The principle is to learn the closure term $\mathbf{S}_\theta$ in

$$
\frac{\partial \bar{\mathbf{x}}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{dyn}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\mathbf{S}_\theta(\bar{\mathbf{x}}), \tag{7}
$$

where $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is the resolved state, $\mathcal{F}_{\text{dyn}}$ the numerical dynamical core and $\mathbf{S}_\theta$ the learned sub-grid tendency. The chief difficulty is coupled stability: a closure that is accurate offline can destabilise the model when its errors feed back through the dynamics, and a learned scheme trained on one climate may misrepresent another [@rasp2018; @reichstein].

### 7.4 Physics-informed neural networks

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) enforce differential equations through the training loss [@raissi]. Consider transport of a scalar $C$, for example a pollutant or tracer, by a known wind field $\mathbf{u}$:

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla C=D\nabla^{2}C+S. \tag{8}
$$

Let $\hat{C}(x,y,z,t;\theta)$ be a network approximation. The PDE residual is

$$
R(x,y,z,t;\theta)=\frac{\partial \hat{C}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\hat{C}-D\nabla^{2}\hat{C}-S, \tag{9}
$$

evaluated by automatic differentiation at $N_r$ collocation points. The training loss combines data misfit, residual and boundary or initial-condition terms:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{w_d}{N_d}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\big|\hat{C}(\mathbf{x}_i,t_i;\theta)-C_i\big|^{2}+\frac{w_p}{N_r}\sum_{j=1}^{N_r}\big|R(\mathbf{x}_j,t_j;\theta)\big|^{2}+\frac{w_b}{N_b}\sum_{l=1}^{N_b}\big|\mathcal{B}[\hat{C}](\mathbf{x}_l,t_l)-g_l\big|^{2}. \tag{10}
$$

Figure 3 shows how the terms are assembled. The same machinery solves inverse problems: if $D$ or $S$ is unknown it can be added to the trainable parameters and inferred from sparse concentration measurements [@raissi]. In practice the weights $w_d$, $w_p$ and $w_b$ strongly influence convergence, and PINNs become difficult to train for stiff, chaotic or highly multiscale problems, which includes most atmospheric flows at full complexity [@kashinath].

### 7.5 AI-assisted numerical weather and climate models

Learned global weather models are the most visible application. FourCastNet uses adaptive Fourier neural operators to forecast global fields at 0.25° resolution orders of magnitude faster than a conventional ensemble [@pathak]. GraphCast uses a graph neural network on a multi-scale icosahedral mesh and was reported to outperform the operational high-resolution deterministic system of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts on a large majority of verification targets for 10-day forecasts [@lam]. Both are trained on ERA5 and therefore reproduce the statistics of that reanalysis [@hersbach]. Two cautions apply. First, these models are validated at weather time scales, where initial-value predictability matters; their behaviour over decades under changing forcing is a separate and unresolved question. Second, they provide a single deterministic trajectory unless extended with perturbations or generative components, so ensemble and probabilistic capability must be added deliberately.

### 7.6 Surrogate modelling

Surrogates, or emulators, approximate the input–output map of an expensive model. A climate emulator can learn the response of regional temperature and precipitation to forcing scenarios from a modest number of CMIP6 runs and then evaluate arbitrary scenarios in seconds [@eyring; @nguyen]. Emulators are valuable for integrated-assessment and impact studies, which require thousands of scenario evaluations. Their validity is bounded by the range of forcings in the training ensemble, and extrapolation beyond that range must be flagged.

### 7.7 Data assimilation with machine learning

Machine learning enters data assimilation in several ways: as a fast surrogate for the forecast model inside ensemble methods, as a learned observation operator mapping model state to satellite radiances, as a bias-correction scheme for model error, and as a replacement for parts of the analysis step. Because reanalyses such as ERA5 are themselves products of assimilation [@hersbach], improved assimilation improves the training targets of every data-driven model. This connection suggests tight coupling of learning and assimilation, in which learned models forecast and are corrected by observations in a continual loop.

## 8. Explainable AI for Climate Science

### 8.1 Why interpretability matters

A forecast that is accurate but unexplained invites distrust from practitioners and, more seriously, hides failure. A network may achieve high skill by exploiting an artefact, such as a satellite-era discontinuity or a spurious correlation, and no accuracy metric on in-distribution data will reveal it. Explainability methods are tools for scientific scrutiny: they allow a researcher to ask whether the model's reasoning agrees with known physics, and to discover when it does not [@reichstein].

### 8.2 Feature importance

Global importance ranks predictors by their overall contribution. Permutation importance measures the drop in skill when a predictor's values are shuffled; tree-based importances measure impurity reduction. Both are easy to compute and both mislead when predictors are correlated, since a shuffled predictor may be partly replaced by its correlated neighbours, spreading or hiding importance. In gridded climate data, where adjacent cells and related variables are strongly correlated, importance should be computed for physically meaningful groups of features.

### 8.3 SHAP and related approaches

SHAP assigns each input a contribution derived from Shapley values of cooperative game theory, giving a unique additive attribution that satisfies local accuracy, missingness and consistency [@lundberg]. For a model $f$ and input $\mathbf{x}$ with feature set $F$,

$$
f(\mathbf{x})=\phi_0+\sum_{i\in F}\phi_i,\qquad
\phi_i=\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\Big[f_{S\cup\{i\}}(\mathbf{x}_{S\cup\{i\}})-f_S(\mathbf{x}_S)\Big], \tag{11}
$$

where $\phi_0$ is the baseline expectation. KernelSHAP estimates the values by weighted linear regression over sampled feature subsets and is model-agnostic. DeepSHAP combines DeepLIFT-style back-propagation with Shapley values for neural networks, and TreeSHAP computes exact values for tree ensembles efficiently [@lundberg]. Integrated gradients attribute the prediction by integrating the gradient along a straight path from a baseline $\mathbf{x}'$ to the input [@sundararajan]:

$$
\operatorname{IG}_i(\mathbf{x})=(x_i-x_i')\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f\big(\mathbf{x}'+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')\big)}{\partial x_i}\,d\alpha. \tag{12}
$$

IG satisfies completeness, in that attributions sum to $f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}')$, and sensitivity. Both families depend on a baseline: the choice of reference climatology or zero field changes what an attribution means.

### 8.4 Local versus global explanations

Local explanations account for one prediction, for example why a model forecast extreme rainfall for a particular storm. Global explanations describe average behaviour across a dataset. The two are linked, since a global importance can be computed as the mean absolute local attribution, but they answer different questions. In climate science local explanations support case studies of individual extremes, while global ones support statements about which processes the model has learned to rely on.

### 8.5 Physical interpretation of learned relationships

An attribution map is a hypothesis, not a finding. If a model forecasting European heatwaves assigns high attribution to soil-moisture deficit and blocking-related geopotential anomalies, the result is consistent with established mechanisms and raises confidence. If it assigns it to a variable with no plausible pathway, the explanation signals either a discovery to be tested or a shortcut the model is exploiting. Composite maps of attribution for a class of events, compared with composite physical anomalies, provide a systematic check.

### 8.6 Avoiding misleading explanations

Attribution methods can be unreliable. They depend on baselines, can be unstable to small input changes, and conflate correlation with causation because they describe the model, not the world. Correlated predictors can split credit arbitrarily, and different methods applied to one model can disagree. Good practice is to use several methods, to check that attributions pass sanity tests such as degrading when model weights are randomised, to test explanations by intervention, and to avoid causal language.
