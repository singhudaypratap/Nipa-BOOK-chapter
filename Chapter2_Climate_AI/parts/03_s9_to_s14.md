## 9. Uncertainty and Reliability

### 9.1 Sources of uncertainty in climate prediction

Climate prediction is uncertain for three reasons that must be kept distinct. *Internal variability* arises from the chaotic nature of the system, so that nearly identical initial states diverge. *Scenario uncertainty* arises from unknown future emissions [@eyring]. *Model uncertainty* arises from imperfect representation of processes. In the language of statistical learning, uncertainty is split into *aleatoric* uncertainty, the irreducible noise in the data-generating process, and *epistemic* uncertainty, which reflects limited knowledge of the model and shrinks with more data. For a predictive distribution with mean $\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ and variance $\sigma_\theta^2(\mathbf{x})$, and an ensemble of $M$ models, the total predictive variance decomposes as

$$
\operatorname{Var}[y\mid\mathbf{x}]=\underbrace{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_m^{2}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{aleatoric}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\big(\mu_m(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{2}}_{\text{epistemic}}, \tag{13}
$$

where $\bar{\mu}=M^{-1}\sum_m\mu_m$ [@lakshminarayanan]. The distinction has practical consequences: epistemic uncertainty flags inputs unlike the training data and can be reduced by collecting more of them, whereas aleatoric uncertainty sets the floor on achievable skill.

### 9.2 Data uncertainty

Observations and reanalyses carry measurement error, representativeness error and, for reanalysis, model-dependent bias. ERA5 provides an ensemble-based uncertainty estimate precisely to support users who need to propagate this error [@hersbach]. Training on a single deterministic product treats it as truth, so the model's skill is conditional on that product. Where possible, training and evaluation should use several independent products and report the spread.

### 9.3 Model uncertainty

Model uncertainty includes structural error in numerical models, evident in the spread across CMIP6 members [@eyring], and for learned models it includes the arbitrary choices of architecture, hyperparameters, random initialisation and training sample. Retraining the same architecture from different seeds can give different predictions in regions of sparse data, and this seed-to-seed variation is a direct, if partial, measure of epistemic uncertainty.

### 9.4 Predictive uncertainty

Predictive uncertainty is what the user ultimately needs: a distribution over the quantity of interest, conditional on the inputs. For a heteroscedastic Gaussian head, training minimises the negative log-likelihood

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\big(y_i-\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^2}{2\sigma_\theta^2(\mathbf{x}_i)}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\sigma_\theta^2(\mathbf{x}_i)\right], \tag{14}
$$

which lets the network predict larger variance where it is less certain. A Gaussian is a poor description of precipitation and many extremes, so skewed or mixture distributions, or direct quantile prediction, are frequently needed.

### 9.5 Ensemble approaches

Deep ensembles train $M$ networks from different random initialisations, optionally on resampled data, and combine their predictions as a uniformly weighted mixture; they have been shown to give well-calibrated uncertainty that often matches or beats approximate Bayesian methods, at the cost of $M$ trainings [@lakshminarayanan]. In weather forecasting, ensembles are also generated by perturbing initial conditions and, for learned models, by injecting noise during rollout. Ensemble spread should be tested against actual error: a well-calibrated ensemble has a spread-skill ratio near one, and ensembles of weather models are commonly under-dispersive.

### 9.6 Probabilistic forecasting

Bayesian neural networks place a distribution over weights and compute the posterior predictive by integrating over it, which is intractable for deep networks and is approximated variationally or by sampling. Monte Carlo dropout interprets dropout at test time as approximate variational inference, so that repeated stochastic forward passes yield a predictive distribution at little extra cost [@gal]. Its estimates can be overconfident and depend on the dropout rate, which limits its reliability. Evidential deep learning instead predicts the parameters of a higher-order distribution over the likelihood parameters in a single pass; it is cheap, but its uncertainty estimates have been found to be sensitive to regularisation and are not guaranteed to be calibrated. For climate applications, the safest course is to treat any single method's uncertainty as provisional until verified on independent data.

### 9.7 Calibration and reliability assessment

A probabilistic forecast is *reliable* if events forecast with probability $p$ occur with frequency $p$. A reliability diagram bins forecast probabilities and plots the observed frequency in each bin against the bin's mean forecast probability, and points on the diagonal indicate perfect calibration. Figure 4 shows the construction with illustrative data. Proper scoring rules assess calibration and sharpness together; the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is the integrated squared difference between the forecast cumulative distribution function $F$ and the step function at the observation $y$ [@gneiting]:

$$
\operatorname{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F(z)-\mathbb{1}\{z\ge y\}\big)^{2}\,dz, \tag{15}
$$

and for an ensemble of $M$ members $\{x_m\}$ can be estimated by $\frac{1}{M}\sum_m|x_m-y|-\frac{1}{2M^{2}}\sum_{m,n}|x_m-x_n|$. CRPS reduces to the absolute error for a deterministic forecast, so it permits direct comparison of deterministic and probabilistic systems. Because it is a proper score, it is minimised in expectation only by the true predictive distribution [@gneiting].

## 10. Evaluating AI-Based Climate Models

### 10.1 Training, validation and independent testing

Data are split into training, validation and test sets, the first for fitting, the second for choosing hyperparameters and stopping, and the third, touched once, for the final estimate. The test set must be independent in the sense that matters for the application. Splitting climate data randomly in time gives neighbouring days to training and test sets, and because consecutive days are strongly autocorrelated the test set then reproduces the training set; skill estimated this way is optimistic. Splits should be contiguous blocks, ideally by year.

### 10.2 Spatial and temporal cross-validation

Block cross-validation withholds contiguous blocks, in time, in space or both, with buffers between training and test blocks wider than the autocorrelation length. Spatial block cross-validation withholds whole regions and estimates how a model will perform where it has no data, which is the question that matters for transferability (Section 6.6). Leave-one-year-out and leave-one-model-out schemes, the latter withholding an entire climate model, test generalisation across years and across model structures. The block size should be set from the empirical variogram or autocorrelation function of the residuals.

### 10.3 Regression and classification metrics

Regression is scored by root-mean-square error, $\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_i w_i(f_i-o_i)^2}$ with area weights $w_i\propto\cos\phi_i$, and by mean absolute error, which is less sensitive to outliers. Event forecasts are scored from the contingency table of hits $a$, false alarms $b$, misses $c$ and correct negatives $d$: the probability of detection is $a/(a+c)$, the false-alarm ratio $b/(a+b)$, and the threat score (critical success index) $a/(a+b+c)$. For rare events, accuracy $(a+d)/N$ is uninformative because the correct-negative count dominates. Probabilistic event forecasts are scored with the Brier score, the mean squared difference between forecast probability and the binary outcome, which is a proper score [@gneiting].

### 10.4 Forecast skill

Skill is always relative to a reference. The skill score $\mathrm{SS}=1-\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{forecast}}/\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ measures improvement over climatology or persistence. The anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) measures the pattern agreement between forecast and observed anomalies relative to climatology:

$$
\mathrm{ACC}=\frac{\sum_i w_i\,(f_i-\bar{f})(o_i-\bar{o})}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i\,(f_i-\bar{f})^{2}\;\sum_i w_i\,(o_i-\bar{o})^{2}}}, \tag{16}
$$

where $f_i$ and $o_i$ are forecast and observed anomalies, with anomalies defined relative to the same climatology as in Equation (1) (so that $\bar f$ and $\bar o$ are area-weighted spatial means of the anomalies), and $w_i$ are area weights. ACC is insensitive to systematic bias in amplitude, so it must be reported alongside RMSE. A common practical threshold is an ACC of 0.6, below which a forecast is usually considered to have lost useful skill relative to climatology, and the lead time at which ACC drops below it is a headline measure in the weather-forecast benchmarks used by WeatherBench and GraphCast [@rasp2020; @lam].

### 10.5 Extreme-event performance

Average metrics hide tail behaviour. A model can achieve low RMSE while systematically underpredicting the largest values. Tail performance should be assessed with metrics conditioned on extremes: error restricted to values above a high percentile, quantile scores at the 95th and 99th percentiles, threat scores at extreme thresholds, and comparison of the upper tail of the predicted and observed distributions using quantile–quantile plots. Extreme value theory can provide return-level estimates against which a model's extrapolation is judged. Because events are rare, confidence intervals from bootstrap resampling in blocks are essential.

### 10.6 Physical consistency

Skill scores do not test whether predictions obey physics. Complementary diagnostics include the global budget of mass, water and energy over long rollouts, the spectral energy distribution (a model that is too smooth will lose energy at small scales), the balance relations such as geostrophic and hydrostatic balance, and the preservation of known correlations between variables. A model whose RMSE is competitive but whose kinetic-energy spectrum collapses at high wavenumbers is blurring, not forecasting [@kashinath].

### 10.7 Generalization to unseen climate regimes

The ultimate test for climate applications is performance in a climate unlike the training period. Proxy tests include training on early decades and testing on recent ones, training on a subset of climate models or scenarios and testing on withheld ones, and training on cool-climate regions and testing on warm ones [@reichstein]. These tests can reveal whether the model has learned stable physical relationships or period-specific correlations. Perfect-model experiments, in which a climate model is treated as truth, allow evaluation under a warmer climate for which no observations exist, with the caveat that the model's own errors define the truth.

## 11. Climate Change Mitigation and Adaptation

### 11.1 Energy and emissions forecasting

Forecasts of electricity demand, net load and grid carbon intensity underpin demand-side flexibility and storage dispatch. Learned models combine weather forecasts with calendar and consumption data, and the accuracy of the weather input, particularly of temperature and cloud cover, is often the dominant error source [@rolnick]. The same forecasts support carbon-aware scheduling of flexible loads.

### 11.2 Renewable-energy prediction

Wind and solar output depend directly on near-surface wind and irradiance, so improved weather prediction translates into improved generation forecasts. Learned global models offer fast, probabilistic input ensembles for such forecasting [@pathak; @lam], and statistical post-processing corrects local biases. Because the cost of forecast error is asymmetric, since imbalance penalties and reserve costs differ in sign, quantile and probabilistic forecasts, scored by CRPS or pinball loss, are more useful to operators than point forecasts [@gneiting].

### 11.3 Carbon-emission modelling

Machine learning supports bottom-up estimation of emissions from activity data and top-down inversion of fluxes from satellite and ground observations of concentrations. For inversion, a transport model links surface fluxes to observed concentrations, and learned surrogates of the transport model accelerate the estimation. The result is an uncertain estimate that must carry its uncertainty into any policy accounting [@rolnick].

### 11.4 Water-resource management

Seasonal streamflow, snowpack and reservoir-inflow forecasts support allocation and flood control. Downscaled precipitation and temperature projections [@vandal; @cannon] drive hydrological impact models, and drought indices such as SPEI frame the risk [@vicente]. Decision-makers value forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, which allows them to weigh the cost of acting against the probability and severity of the event.

### 11.5 Agricultural adaptation

Crop-yield models driven by seasonal forecasts, soil moisture and satellite vegetation indices guide planting, irrigation and insurance. Tree ensembles are widely used here because they cope with mixed tabular inputs [@breiman; @chen]. A distinct need is projecting yields under future climate, where models trained on historical relationships must extrapolate, and this is the setting in which hybrid crop-physiology models are most defensible.

### 11.6 Climate-risk assessment

Risk combines hazard, exposure and vulnerability. AI contributes to the hazard component through improved event statistics, downscaled projections and large synthetic ensembles of extremes, and to exposure and vulnerability through remote-sensing-based mapping of assets and populations. Compound and cascading risks require joint modelling of hazards, for which learned multivariate distributions are useful, but the extreme-value dependence structure they must capture is data-hungry.

### 11.7 Early-warning systems

Early-warning systems translate forecasts into actionable alerts. Their value depends on lead time, on calibrated probabilities that support threshold-based action, and on communication. Fast learned forecasts allow larger ensembles within operational time limits and so better probability estimates [@pathak; @lam]. A warning system should be evaluated by decision outcomes, including false-alarm cost and missed-event cost, not forecast skill alone.

## 12. Limitations, Risks and Responsible Climate AI

### 12.1 Data scarcity and geographical imbalance

Observations are densest where wealth is greatest, so models trained on them perform best for regions that are already best served, and may fail for regions most vulnerable to climate impacts [@rolnick]. Reanalysis fills gaps but with model-dependent quality in poorly observed regions [@hersbach]. Reporting regional performance, not global averages alone, is the minimum safeguard.

### 12.2 Dataset bias

Biases are inherited from the training targets. A model trained on a reanalysis reproduces its precipitation bias; a model trained on a single climate model reproduces its sensitivity [@hersbach; @eyring]. Training on multi-model ensembles reduces dependence on any one structure, but ensembles are not independent because models share code and parameterisations.

### 12.3 Overfitting and spurious correlations

With short records and many predictors, flexible models readily fit noise. Climate time series are autocorrelated, so the effective sample size is much smaller than the number of time steps, and ordinary cross-validation overstates generalisation. Remedies are block validation (Section 10.2), regularisation, physically motivated predictor selection and comparison with simple baselines.

### 12.4 Extrapolation beyond the training climate

Climate change is by definition a shift in the distribution of inputs. A model that has never seen temperatures or moisture states above those in its training record may behave unpredictably, and tree-based methods cannot extrapolate at all (Section 3.2). Physics-informed constraints and hybrid designs retain correct behaviour more reliably out of distribution [@reichstein; @kashinath]. Epistemic uncertainty estimates should be used to flag out-of-distribution inputs, with the caveat that they are themselves unreliable far from training data.

### 12.5 Interpretability

Opaque models are difficult to audit. Explanation methods help but are imperfect (Section 8.6) and should be treated as hypotheses to be tested, not as evidence of mechanism [@lundberg; @sundararajan].

### 12.6 Reproducibility

Deep-learning results depend on random seeds, software versions, hardware and preprocessing details. Reproducibility requires published code, fixed data splits, documented normalisation statistics and reporting of results across multiple seeds with spread, in the manner that benchmarks such as WeatherBench make possible [@rasp2020].

### 12.7 Computational and energy costs of AI

Training large models consumes substantial energy, and a model that saves compute at inference after a large training cost must be used enough to amortise it. Rolnick et al. note that machine learning has its own emissions that should be weighed against its benefits [@rolnick]. Efficient architectures, transfer from pre-trained models [@nguyen] and reporting of training compute are practical responses.

### 12.8 Physical plausibility and scientific trust

Trust in a climate model rests on understanding why it works. Data-driven systems can earn trust through long-rollout stability, conservation diagnostics, correct response in perturbation experiments and transparent uncertainty [@kashinath; @reichstein]. They should be regarded as complements to, not replacements for, physics-based models until their behaviour under changed forcing has been demonstrated.

## 13. Emerging Directions

### 13.1 Foundation models for Earth-system science

Foundation models are pre-trained on large and diverse data and then fine-tuned for many tasks. ClimaX is a transformer pre-trained on CMIP6 data with variable tokenisation and aggregation, allowing it to handle heterogeneous variables and resolutions and to be fine-tuned for forecasting, downscaling and climate projection [@nguyen]. Such models promise reuse and data efficiency, but their generalisation to novel regimes, and the biases they inherit from the pre-training simulations, need systematic study.

### 13.2 Multimodal climate AI

Future systems will fuse gridded fields, satellite imagery, station time series, text reports and socio-economic data. Multimodal fusion raises the alignment problems of Section 2.6 and the risk that a model learns to rely on whichever modality is most convenient, not most informative.

### 13.3 AI-assisted Earth-system modelling

Learned components are entering operational Earth-system models as parameterisations, bias correctors and fast emulators of sub-models [@rasp2018; @reichstein]. The research priority is stable, conservative coupling that remains valid under climate change.

### 13.4 Physics–AI co-modelling

Beyond hybrids that replace single components, co-modelling treats physical and learned models as partners that exchange information during training and inference, with constraints and differentiable solvers permitting end-to-end optimisation [@kashinath; @raissi]. Differentiable physics enables gradient-based calibration of parameters against observations.

### 13.5 Probabilistic and uncertainty-aware AI

Generative models, including diffusion and score-based approaches, offer a route to large calibrated ensembles at low cost, and could address the under-dispersion of deterministic learned forecasters [@lakshminarayanan; @gneiting]. Verification with proper scores must accompany them.

### 13.6 AI for compound climate hazards

Compound events, such as concurrent heat and drought or storm surge coinciding with heavy rainfall, are hard to study because their joint probabilities need long records. Large emulator ensembles and learned dependence models offer a means of sampling them, provided that tail dependence is validated [@eyring; @nguyen].

### 13.7 Toward decision-oriented climate intelligence

The aim is to move from variables to decisions: systems that couple calibrated forecasts, impact models and cost–loss analysis to recommend actions, with explanation and uncertainty attached [@rolnick]. This requires co-design with the people who use the information.

## 14. Conclusions

AI has become a serious instrument of climate analysis and prediction. Classical learners such as random forests and gradient boosting remain effective for small tabular problems [@breiman; @chen]. Convolutional, recurrent and attention-based networks learn spatial and temporal structure from gridded data [@shi; @hochreiter; @vaswani; @dosovitskiy], and learned global models now produce weather forecasts at a fraction of the cost of numerical ensembles [@pathak; @lam]. Downscaling networks add fine-scale detail to coarse fields, though they add plausible texture as well as information and require careful bias correction [@vandal; @cannon].

The central conclusion of this chapter is that accuracy is not enough. Climate prediction is an extrapolation problem under changing forcing, and models that ignore conservation laws can drift or fail outside their training regime. Hybrid and physics-informed designs, from learned sub-grid parameterisations to PINNs for advection–diffusion, give a principled route to physical consistency [@rasp2018; @raissi; @kashinath]. Explainability tools such as SHAP and integrated gradients help scrutinise models but yield hypotheses, not proofs [@lundberg; @sundararajan]. Calibrated uncertainty, assessed with proper scores such as CRPS and with reliability diagrams, is a requirement, not an extra [@gneiting; @lakshminarayanan].

Evaluation must be a portfolio: block cross-validation, anomaly correlation, extreme-event metrics and physical diagnostics together (Table 3). Responsible practice requires attention to geographic bias, reproducibility, energy cost and the limits of extrapolation [@rolnick]. Foundation models and generative methods open new possibilities [@nguyen], and their use will be most valuable where they are coupled to physical understanding and judged by their contribution to decisions on mitigation and adaptation.
